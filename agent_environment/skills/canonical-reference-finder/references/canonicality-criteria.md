# Canonicality Criteria

Use these criteria as a structured judgment. Do not compute a universal score:
the relative importance depends on the citation's role and field.

## Required gates

A recommended source must:

- address the same narrowly defined topic;
- support the local manuscript claim in inspected full text;
- be correctly identified and attributable; and
- be appropriate for the role assigned to it.

Failure of a gate excludes the work as a verified replacement, regardless of
citation count.

## Evidence to compare

1. **Claim fit** — Does the source directly establish, derive, define, or
   demonstrate the proposition?
2. **Priority** — Is it the earliest defensible source for the contribution?
   Distinguish invention from precursors and independent rediscoveries.
3. **Definitiveness** — Is this the mature formulation, proof, dataset,
   standard, or terminology actually used by the manuscript?
4. **Field recognition** — Do later authoritative works consistently attribute
   the topic to it?
5. **Citation influence** — What is its date-stamped Google Scholar count
   relative to genuinely comparable candidates?
6. **Authority and stability** — Is it peer reviewed, an official standard, a
   respected monograph, or the maintained authoritative specification?
7. **Accessibility and verifiability** — Can readers locate the exact evidence
   in a stable version?
8. **Currency** — Does a newer source materially correct, supersede, or
   consolidate the older one?

## Common traps

- A highly cited review can be canonical background but not the original source.
- An original paper can establish priority yet be a poor source for the modern
  formulation used in the manuscript.
- Older works have more time to accrue citations; very recent definitive work
  may have a lower count.
- Books, standards, software papers, and review articles have different
  citation patterns from research articles.
- Google Scholar may merge versions, duplicate records, or change counts.
- Self-citations, field size, language, discoverability, and naming conventions
  distort raw counts.
- A citation inherited through a chain may be a “sleeping citation”: inspect it
  instead of trusting later attribution.
- “Seminal,” “first,” and “canonical” are historical claims that require
  stronger evidence than topical relevance.

## Recommended outcomes

- **Keep** — the present citation directly supports the claim and is canonical
  for its assigned role.
- **Supplement** — it supports the claim, but an original, definitive, or review
  source is needed for another role.
- **Replace** — it is weaker or noncanonical and a fully verified candidate
  better serves the same role.
- **Remove** — it does not support the claim and adds no necessary context.
- **Unresolved** — missing full text, disputed priority, ambiguous records, or
  insufficient field evidence prevents a responsible judgment.

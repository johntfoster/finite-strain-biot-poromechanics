\begin{abstract}
Pore pressure changes the volume of both the mineral and the pore space in a
saturated porous solid.  Permanent changes in pore volume also affect the
subsequent reversible response.  We derive this pressure coupling by
constructing a common stored energy that reproduces prescribed logarithmic
laws for the drained skeleton and the mineral.  The construction gives one
scalar equation for mineral volume and the conditions under which its
solution is locally unique.  Changing the independent thermodynamic
variable from mineral density to pore pressure identifies the reversible
Biot coefficient in two ways: as the pore-volume change per unit change in
total volume at fixed pressure, and as the pressure sensitivity of total
stress at fixed deformation.  Differentiating the mineral equation gives
an explicit expression for this coefficient and relates the effective
stresses defined at fixed pressure and fixed mineral density.

We also express total stress as the zero-pressure drained stress plus a
pressure contribution determined by mineral compression.  The multiplier
of this finite pressure contribution generally differs from the reversible
Biot coefficient measured through small poroelastic perturbations.
Linearization about the undeformed, zero-pressure state recovers classical
Biot elasticity.  For plastic deformation, we separate changes in mineral
shape that preserve its volume from permanent changes in pore volume.
The numerical update solves for mineral volume and plastic deformation
together.  Automatic differentiation includes their mutual dependence in
the derivatives required by Newton's method.

Independent checks verify mass conservation, the constitutive equations,
and the derivatives used in the numerical solution.  The coupled
finite-element formulation recovers the elastic Mandel solution and shows
reversible changes in the Biot coefficient under larger compression and
drainage.  Calculations with prescribed homogeneous deformation and pressure show how
plastic history changes the coefficient during unloading.  They also show
how mineral compression affects the stress driving further plastic flow.  A poroplastic compression test verifies the plastic
contribution to fluid storage and shows a persistent increase in the
coefficient relative to its elastic value at the same pressure and total
volume.  For the selected synthetic geomaterial, plastic dilation reverses
fluid exchange from outflow to inflow.  These results connect mineral
compression and permanent pore-volume change to evolving pressure
coupling; comparison with material data remains future work.
\end{abstract}

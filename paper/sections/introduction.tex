\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Pore pressure deforms both the pore space and the mineral in a saturated
porous solid.  At finite strain, mineral compressibility couples pressure
to the elastic stress and changes the Biot coefficient as the body deforms.
We derive the coupled stored energy, effective stresses, and a closed-form
Biot coefficient for matched logarithmic skeleton and mineral laws.  The
construction applies to purely elastic deformation and extends to
poroplasticity, where plastic distention carries loading history into the
Biot coefficient and the stress driving further plastic flow.

The Biot coefficient describes how pore volume changes with the volume of
the body when pore pressure is held fixed.  In classical linear elasticity,
it is determined by the ratio of the drained skeleton bulk modulus to the
mineral bulk modulus \citep{biotwillis1957}.  After plastic deformation,
the elastic response can change.  Measurements during sandstone compaction
show this dependence on loading history: the drained modulus is measured
from a small elastic unloading loop, and the mineral modulus from an
unjacketed test \citep{ingraham2017evolution}.  These measurements motivate
evaluating the coefficient from the reversible response at the current
plastic state.  To predict further plastic deformation, we must also
specify the stored energy and the law governing plastic flow.  We examine
the consequences of those choices for a synthetic pressure dependent material.

An energy description connects mineral compression to the stress carried
by the porous body.  Biot's finite-deformation theory uses deformation and
fluid mass as independent variables, allowing fluid to enter or leave a
volume moving with the skeleton.  Derivatives of the energy give the stress
and fluid potential \citep{biot1972}.  Gajo constructs this energy from the
responses of the skeleton, mineral, and pore fluid, including the work
associated with their coupling \citep{gajo2010}.  For logarithmic elastic laws, his energy
gives a mineral-pressure contribution to the total stress, and his rate
equations contain the elastic Biot coefficient obtained here
\citep[Secs.~3(d) and 5]{gajo2010}.

Our derivation begins by separating changes in mineral volume from changes
in pore space.  Drumheller describes this separation through mineral
deformation and distention \citep{drumheller2000}.  In earlier work with
Xu, we used the mineral-volume response at fixed pore pressure to derive
the finite-deformation Biot coefficient \citep{fosterxu2025}.  Here we
construct the stored energy for logarithmic skeleton and mineral laws and
use it to distinguish the stress obtained at fixed pore pressure from that
obtained at fixed mineral density.  The former includes a pressure
correction determined by mineral compression; the latter supplies the
stress that does work on plastic deformation in the model considered here.
The Biot coefficient connects these two stresses.  The elastic total stress
recovers Gajo's logarithmic response, and linearization about the undeformed,
zero-pressure state recovers classical Biot elasticity.

The resulting expression gives the Biot coefficient directly from the
current total volume, pore pressure, and mineral volume.  Mineral volume
must first be found from a scalar equation expressing pressure equilibrium.
In virgin elasticity, this equation is equivalent to Gajo's logarithmic
mineral relations \citep[Eqs.~(3.27), (3.32), and (3.34)]{gajo2010}.
Solid mass conservation then gives the mineral density and solid volume
fraction.  During plastic loading, we solve for the mineral state and
plastic deformation together.  The coefficient describes an elastic
change at a fixed plastic state, while the stress driving further plastic
flow depends on its value at that state.  This distinction allows us to
follow how plastic changes in pore space affect subsequent deformation.

The numerical formulation couples this material response to fluid flow.
Large-deformation models of gels and porous solids describe the interaction
between deformation and fluid transport \citep{hongetal2008,macminnetal2016},
and multiscale models relate changing poroelastic properties to changes in
microstructure \citep{dehghanizilian2021}.  Solving the coupled equations
requires derivatives of the stress calculated by the material update
\citep{simotaylor1985,miehe1996}.  Automatic differentiation computes such
derivatives in finite-strain poromechanics \citep{sunostien2013} and within
MOOSE's finite-element assembly \citep{lindsayetal2021ad}.  We use it to
carry changes in mineral volume, plastic deformation, and the Biot
coefficient into the derivatives used by Newton's method.

We first derive the energy, Biot coefficient, and elastic stress response,
then introduce plastic flow.  Material-point calculations examine loading
and unloading and compare the predicted plastic response when the stress
transformation uses either the current Biot coefficient or its virgin
value.  We then couple the material response to solid and water mass
conservation and momentum balance.  Pressure and displacement profiles are
compared with the Mandel solution to test the elastic limit
\citep{chengdetournay1988}.  Larger compression tests examine how the Biot
coefficient varies through the body during elastic and plastic deformation.
The plastic calculations also check the contribution of changing pore
volume to fluid storage and compare the coefficient with its elastic value
at the same pressure and total volume.  Finally, we examine the stability
of the plastic response and the effect of hardening.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Plastic deformation changes the pore volume of a saturated solid and the
elastic response of its skeleton.  When the mineral is compressible, it also
changes how pore pressure contributes to stress.  The Biot coefficient must
then follow the constitutive state through loading and unloading.  Its value
enters the stress that drives further plastic flow, so the Biot coefficient
and the plastic state must be evaluated together.  We derive a closed-form
finite-deformation Biot coefficient for matched logarithmic skeleton and
mineral constitutive laws, incorporate it into an implicit poroplastic
update, and verify the constitutive and finite-element derivatives.

The distinction between mineral volume and pore volume is central to the
construction.  Drumheller separates the true deformation of a mineral
constituent, which changes the volume of its own material, from the
distention that describes pore volume allocation
\citep{drumheller2000}.  In
earlier work with Xu, we expressed the finite-deformation Biot coefficient as
the change of intrinsic solid volume with respect to changes in skeleton volume at fixed pore
pressure \citep{fosterxu2025}.  That relation connects the effective
stress evaluated at fixed pore pressure, written with a double prime, to the
effective stress evaluated at fixed solid density, written with a single
prime.  For the constitutive model considered here, the fixed-density stress
is the stress that does work on plastic deformation.  Evolution of the Biot
coefficient therefore affects both the total mixture stress and the
evaluation of yield and flow.

The reference Biot coefficient recovers the familiar relation between skeleton
and mineral bulk moduli \citep{biotwillis1957}.  At a plastically deformed
state, its value depends on the current elastic response and stored
distention.  Measurements during sandstone compaction give experimental
motivation for that state dependence \citep{ingraham2017evolution}.  Those
measurements report the Biot coefficient from an elastic unloading response:
the drained bulk modulus is taken from the slope of a small unloading loop at
the current mean stress, and the unjacketed mineral grain modulus from an
unjacketed test.  The reported value is therefore the reversible change of
pore volume at fixed inelastic state, the same fixed-state derivative that
defines the Biot coefficient here.  Converting that derivative into a stress
that drives plastic flow requires a plastic energy and a flow law, and the
choice among them sets the pressure feedback examined below.  We therefore
state those assumptions explicitly and report their consequences for a
synthetic material rather than fitting the response of a particular rock.

The closed-form expression gives the Biot coefficient from the current volume,
pore pressure, and mineral state.  A scalar mineral equation and solid mass
conservation determine the intrinsic mineral density and the volume fraction
at each plastic state.  The implicit return mapping advances plastic
deformation and updates the Biot coefficient and the driving stress with it.
The derivative that defines the Biot coefficient holds the plastic state
fixed, so it describes the reversible unloading response.  The driving stress
contains the Biot coefficient, so differentiating the stress with respect to
the deformation must also carry the change in the coefficient that
accompanies a change in the plastic state.

This construction connects established developments in coupled
porous-material simulation and constitutive linearization.  Large-deformation
models of gels and porous solids describe the coupling between material
deformation and fluid transport \citep{hongetal2008,macminnetal2016}, and
multiscale formulations evolve effective poroelastic properties with
microstructure \citep{dehghanizilian2021}; the Biot coefficient derived here is
a related state-dependent poroelastic property.  Consistent tangents
differentiate the discrete material update used by the nonlinear solver
\citep{simotaylor1985,miehe1996}.  Automatic differentiation (AD) supplies
coupled finite-strain poromechanics Jacobians from residual evaluations
\citep{sunostien2013}, and MOOSE supports AD-valued local calculations within
its finite-element assembly \citep{lindsayetal2021ad}.  Here the mineral
equation determines the Biot coefficient, and AD includes its dependence on
the evolving plastic state in the Newton Jacobian.

We first establish the kinematics and pressure transformation, then derive
the Biot coefficient and specify the plastic flow law.  Material-point
calculations examine loading history, unloading, and pressure feedback.
The constitutive response is coupled to solid and water mass conservation
and momentum balance.  Spatial pressure and displacement comparisons with
the Mandel solution test the elastic limit of the finite-element formulation
\citep{chengdetournay1988}.  Larger compression tests examine the spatial
Biot coefficient in the elastic and plastic regimes.  The poroplastic test
verifies the plastic pore-volume contribution to fluid storage and compares
the coefficient with its elastic value at matched local pressure and total
volume.  A local acoustic-tensor diagnostic identifies loss of ellipticity
in the perfectly plastic specialization; isotropic hardening supplies a
stable spatial response over the tested path.

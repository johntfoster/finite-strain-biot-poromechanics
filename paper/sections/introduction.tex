\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Pore pressure deforms both the pore space and the mineral in a saturated
porous solid.  The Biot coefficient measures the reversible change in pore
volume with the volume of the body at fixed pore pressure.  In classical
linear elasticity, it is determined by the ratio of the drained skeleton
bulk modulus to the mineral bulk modulus \citep{biotwillis1957}.  At finite
strain, mineral compression and permanent changes in pore space affect
this coupling.  Measurements of sandstone during compaction, using
unload--reload loops and unjacketed tests, show that the inferred coefficient
varies with loading \citep{ingraham2017evolution}.  A constitutive description
must therefore distinguish the reversible response at the current plastic
state from the evolution of that state during further plastic flow.

The central contribution of this work is a derivation that connects the
stored energy, mineral equation of state, and measurable reversible Biot
coefficient for prescribed logarithmic skeleton and mineral laws.  We
construct a common energy that reproduces both laws and reduce mineral
pressure equilibrium to one scalar equation.  We then change the independent
thermodynamic variable from mineral density to pore pressure by a Legendre
transformation.  This step identifies the same Biot coefficient through
pore-volume change, the relation between effective stresses, and the
pressure derivative of total stress.  Differentiating the scalar mineral
equation gives an explicit expression for the coefficient.  Its derivative
also identifies the range in which the mineral solution is locally unique.

A second result separates the pressure contribution to a finite stress
change from the coefficient inferred through small reversible experiments.
We express total stress relative to the drained stress at zero pressure,
with total and plastic deformation held fixed.  The pressure multiplier
in this relation describes the complete stress change from zero pressure;
the reversible Biot coefficient describes the slope at the current state.
For plastic deformation, the same construction shows how permanent changes
in pore volume alter subsequent reversible pressure coupling.  Two states
with the same total volume and pore pressure can therefore have different
Biot coefficients because of their loading histories.  We demonstrate this
history dependence during unloading and reloading.  The numerical
contribution is a coupled update of mineral volume and plastic deformation,
with independent checks of its derivatives, fluid storage, and
finite-element response.

An energy description connects mineral compression to the stress carried
by the porous body.  Biot's finite-deformation theory uses deformation and
fluid mass as independent variables, allowing fluid to enter or leave a
volume moving with the skeleton.  Derivatives of the energy give the stress
and fluid potential \citep{biot1972}.  Gajo constructs this energy from the
responses of the skeleton, mineral, and pore fluid, including the work
associated with their coupling \citep{gajo2010}.  His logarithmic elastic
formulation describes mineral compression through separate deformation
factors associated with contact loading and pore pressure.  Gajo's
extension to finite-strain plasticity includes plastic-state dependence
and treats reversible mineral volume change as a particular case
\citep{gajo2011}.  For reversible mineral volume change and the logarithmic
constitutive choices used here, eliminating his contact-loading and
pressure-loading factors recovers our
scalar mineral equation, including its dependence on plastic distention
\citep[Eqs.~(35)--(39), (47), and Appendix~C.1]{gajo2011}.
Gajo's finite stress multiplier and rate coupling provide the constitutive
foundation for this comparison.  Our derivation uses the pressure Legendre
transformation to identify the reversible coefficient and connect its
experimental meaning to the scalar constitutive update.  We give the elastic
correspondence explicitly in \cref{sec:gajo-elastic-equivalence}.

Plastic loading also changes the incremental pressure response through
the active plastic flow.  \citet{suvorovselvadurai2019} derive and plot an
evolving tangent Biot coefficient for a composite-sphere model by
eliminating the plastic-strain increment through the consistency condition.
Here the Biot coefficient is evaluated with the plastic configuration
held fixed.  Comparing this reversible coefficient at matched total volume
and pore pressure isolates the effect of accumulated plastic deformation
on subsequent elastic pressure coupling.  The plastic flow law determines how that
constitutive state evolves.  Drained and unjacketed measurements, including
elastic unloading moduli, characterize this reversible response
\citep{makhnenkolabuz2016}; a mechanical unload--reload loop alone supplies
a stiffness rather than a complete measurement of pressure coupling.

Our derivation begins by separating changes in mineral volume from changes
in pore space.  Drumheller describes this separation through mineral
deformation and distention \citep{drumheller2000}.  In earlier work with
Xu, we used the mineral-volume response at fixed pore pressure to derive
the finite-deformation Biot coefficient \citep{fosterxu2025}.  Here we
use phase-stress averaging and reversible work to construct the stored
energy from the prescribed drained skeleton and mineral laws.  The resulting
scalar pressure-equilibrium equation supplies mineral density, solid volume
fraction, and the fixed-pressure tangent from a common constitutive state.
Differentiating the solid energy with respect to deformation at fixed pore
pressure gives an effective stress that includes the effect of mineral
compression.  Taking the deformation derivative at fixed mineral density
gives the effective stress that does work on plastic deformation in this
model.  The Legendre transformation relates these two stresses through the
Biot coefficient.  Expressing both relative to the zero-pressure drained
stress then separates the finite pressure contribution from the local
reversible response.  Both follow from the same mineral state.
Linearization about the undeformed, zero-pressure state recovers classical
Biot elasticity.

The scalar mineral equation has a unique positive root at nonnegative
pressure.  At negative pressure, more than one root can exist.  We follow
the solution continuously from zero pressure and retain it while the
derivative of the residual with respect to mineral volume remains positive.
This condition permits both the mineral volume and its sensitivity to
loading to be evaluated along the selected solution.
During plastic loading, we solve for mineral volume and plastic deformation
together, retaining their influence on the stress driving further flow.

The numerical formulation couples this material response to fluid flow.
Large-deformation models of gels and porous solids describe the interaction
between deformation and fluid transport \citep{hongetal2008,macminnetal2016},
and multiscale models relate changing poroelastic properties to changes in
microstructure \citep{dehghanizilian2021}.  Solving the coupled equations
requires derivatives of the stress calculated by the material update
\citep{simotaylor1985,miehe1996}.  Automatic differentiation computes such
derivatives in finite-strain poromechanics \citep{sunostien2013} and within
MOOSE's finite-element assembly \citep{lindsayetal2021ad}.  We use it to
carry changes in mineral volume, plastic deformation, and the Biot
coefficient into the derivatives used by Newton's method.

We first derive the energy, Biot coefficient, and elastic stress response,
then introduce plastic flow.  Material-point calculations examine loading
and unloading and compare the predicted plastic response when the stress
transformation uses either the current Biot coefficient or its virgin
value.  We then couple the material response to solid and water mass
conservation and momentum balance.  Pressure and displacement profiles are
compared with the Mandel solution to test the elastic limit
\citep{chengdetournay1988}.  Larger compression tests examine how the Biot
coefficient varies through the body during elastic and plastic deformation.
The plastic calculations use a synthetic geomaterial with
pressure-dependent yielding and check the contribution of changing pore
volume to fluid storage.  They also compare the coefficient with its
elastic value at the same pressure and total volume.  Finally, we examine
the stability of the plastic response and the effect of hardening.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

We derived a scalar mineral equation of state by matching logarithmic
drained skeleton and mineral responses within a common stored energy.
Phase-stress averaging and reversible work determine the coupling between
mineral compression and distention.  The resulting scalar equation
determines mineral volume.  Its derivative
identifies the range in which that solution is locally unique and gives
the change in mineral volume per unit change in total volume at fixed
pressure.  This derivative determines the finite-deformation Biot
coefficient.  The pressure Legendre transformation identifies this
Biot coefficient through reversible pore-volume change, the effective-stress
relation, and the local pressure sensitivity of total stress.  Eliminating
Gajo's mineral deformation factors establishes the correspondence with his
logarithmic elastic response and, for reversible mineral volume change,
with the volumetric closure in his plastic extension \citep{gajo2010,gajo2011}.

The reduced finite stress relation is
\(\bs\sigma=\bs\sigma^{\prime\prime}_d-B_d p\mbf I\).
The drained stress retains the current plastic history and is independent
of pressure.  The finite multiplier \(B_d\) depends on mineral volume,
while the local reversible Biot coefficient satisfies
\(B=B_d+p\left.\partial B_d/\partial p\right|_{\mbf F,\mbf F^p}\).
The latter is the Biot coefficient to compare with incremental poroelastic
measurements.  The derivation from the pressure potential and the scalar
mineral equation
makes this distinction explicit: the finite stress difference and the local
reversible slope follow from the same stored energy and mineral state.
Linearization about the undeformed, zero-pressure state
recovers classical Biot elasticity with \(B_0=1-K/K_s\).  These results
establish the elastic constitutive construction before plastic distention
is introduced to describe permanent changes in pore volume.

Plastic distention carries loading history into the Biot coefficient.  The
material-point calculations show that unloading follows a different path
from the virgin response at the same total deformation.  At nonzero
pressure, the reduced single-prime stress combines the drained response
with the mineral-compression contribution.  The same result follows from
the double-prime stress and its Biot transformation.  Using
the current Biot coefficient produces less dilation than substituting the virgin
coefficient only in that transformation for the selected loading paths.
This comparison measures sensitivity to the modified transformation.
The constitutive update solves for plastic deformation and mineral volume
together while enforcing the yield condition.  Automatic differentiation
carries their mutual dependence into the derivatives used by Newton's
method.  Independent checks of mass conservation, the stress obtained from
the energy, the flow rule, satisfaction of the yield condition, and the derivatives verify the update on the smooth
part of the Drucker--Prager yield surface.

The coupled finite-element calculations recover the elastic Mandel pressure
and horizontal-displacement solution.  At larger elastic compression, the
Biot coefficient changes reversibly with deformation and drainage, separating
the finite-strain elastic response from the additional effect of plastic
history.  The poroplastic calculations verify fluid storage during plastic
flow.  Finite differences of the stored water mass independently check the
contribution of plastic pore-volume change.  Comparisons of the assembled
Jacobian with finite differences check the derivatives used by the global
Newton solve.
Water-mass change is also checked against boundary flow, and time-step
refinement reduces the drift introduced by the chain-rule time discretization.

For the selected dilative material, the compression-and-hold calculation
produces a positive plastic-history contribution to the Biot coefficient at
matched pressure and total volume. Dilation reverses fluid exchange from
initial outflow to inflow,
and the Biot coefficient retains its plastic contribution during the hold.
For the perfectly plastic model, the acoustic tensor becomes singular
during the hold, indicating a loss of ellipticity of the mechanical
equations.
With positive isotropic hardening, the same dilation rule gives smooth
spatial fields, and the differences between solutions decrease as the mesh
and time step are refined.
This hardening modulus is a constitutive choice for the synthetic test.

Whether the dilative model reproduces both fluid exchange and Biot-coefficient
evolution in a real material remains an open question. Experiments support
dilation-induced pressure reduction and fluid uptake
\citep{makhnenkolabuz2015,brantut2020}, while measured Biot coefficients also
exhibit stress-path-dependent decreases and stabilization
\citep{ingraham2017evolution}. The increase predicted here must therefore be tested
alongside volumetric deformation and drainage, rather than inferred from
dilatancy alone. Calibration and such joint comparisons, together with
extension to cavitation or desaturation where required, are needed to assess
the physical range of the constitutive specialization.

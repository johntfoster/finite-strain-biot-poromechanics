\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

We derived a coupled stored energy, its effective stresses, and a closed-form
finite-deformation Biot coefficient for a saturated solid with logarithmic
skeleton and mineral laws.  Mineral compression produces a pressure
contribution to the elastic stress, and the fixed-pressure energy derivative
retains a pressure correction in the double-prime effective stress.  The
elastic total-stress response recovers the logarithmic specialization of
\citet{gajo2010}; linearization about the undeformed, zero-pressure state
recovers classical Biot elasticity.  A scalar equation determines mineral
volume, and the Biot coefficient follows from that volume, total volume, and
pore pressure, with \(B_0=1-K/K_s\) at the reference state.

Plastic distention carries loading history into the Biot coefficient.  The
material-point calculations show that unloading follows a different path
from the virgin response at the same total deformation.  At nonzero
pressure, the coupled energy extends the calibrated drained double-prime
response, and the effective-stress relation supplies the driving stress.  Using
the current coefficient produces less dilation than substituting the virgin
coefficient only in that transformation for the selected loading paths.
This comparison measures sensitivity to the modified transformation.
The implicit return
mapping advances these quantities together, and automatic differentiation
retains their coupled change in the Newton Jacobian.  Independent checks of
mass conservation, the energy-derived stress, the flow rule, yield
consistency, and the derivatives verify the update on the smooth
part of the Drucker--Prager yield surface.

The coupled finite-element calculations recover the elastic Mandel pressure
and horizontal-displacement solution.  At larger elastic compression, the
Biot coefficient changes reversibly with deformation and drainage, separating
the finite-strain elastic response from the additional effect of plastic
history.  The poroplastic calculations verify fluid storage during plastic
flow.  Independent differences of water accumulation and assembled-Jacobian
comparisons check the plastic pore-volume contribution and its derivatives.
Water-mass change is also checked against boundary flow, and time-step
refinement reduces the drift introduced by the chain-rule time discretization.

For the selected dilative material, the compression-and-hold calculation
produces a positive plastic-history contribution to the Biot coefficient at
matched pressure and total volume. Dilation reverses fluid exchange from
initial outflow to inflow,
and the coefficient retains its plastic contribution during the hold.
The perfectly plastic specialization loses ellipticity along the hold.
A positive isotropic hardening modulus retains the nonassociated flow rule
and gives smooth spatial fields with decreasing differences under refinement.
This hardening modulus is a constitutive choice for the synthetic test.

Whether the dilative model reproduces both fluid exchange and Biot-coefficient
evolution in a real material remains an open question. Experiments support
dilation-induced pressure reduction and fluid uptake
\citep{makhnenkolabuz2015,brantut2020}, while measured Biot coefficients also
exhibit stress-path-dependent decreases and stabilization
\citep{ingraham2017evolution}. The increase predicted here must therefore be tested
alongside volumetric deformation and drainage, rather than inferred from
dilatancy alone. Calibration and such joint comparisons, together with
extension to cavitation or desaturation where required, are needed to assess
the physical range of the constitutive specialization.

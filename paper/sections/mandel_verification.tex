\section{Coupled finite-element verification}
\label{sec:mandel-verification}

The coupled calculations first verify the small-load elastic limit against the
analytical Mandel solution, then examine finite compression with and without
plastic flow. The finite-deformation comparisons distinguish changes in the
current Biot coefficient from discretization error and constitutive instability.

\subsection{Boundary-value problem}
\label{sec:mandel-boundary-value-problem}

The Mandel problem considers a saturated plane-strain specimen compressed
between impermeable, frictionless rigid platens.  Water drains through the
lateral faces \citep{chengdetournay1988}.  Let \(X\) and \(Y\) denote the
horizontal and vertical reference coordinates, and let \(L\) and \(H\) be the
quarter-domain width and height, respectively.  Symmetry reduces the
calculation to
\begin{equation*}
 \Omega_0=\left\{(X,Y):0\leq X\leq L,\ 0\leq Y\leq H\right\},
 \qquad
 L=1\ \mathrm{m},
 \qquad
 H=0.1\ \mathrm{m}.
 % equation-id: eq:mandel-quarter-domain
\end{equation*}
The mechanical symmetry conditions and drained pressure condition are

% equation-id: eq:mandel-boundary-conditions

 \begin{align*}
  u_x(0,Y,t)&=0,
  &u_y(X,0,t)&=0,
 % equation-id: eq:mandel-symmetry-boundaries
\\
  p(L,Y,t)&=0,
  &\mbf W_f\mathbin{\cdot}\mbf N&=0
  \quad\text{on the remaining fluid boundaries}.
 % equation-id: eq:mandel-fluid-boundaries
 \end{align*}

Here \(u_x\) and \(u_y\) are the components of \(\mbf u\), and \(\mbf N\) is
the outward unit normal to the reference boundary.
For the small-load benchmark, the top displacement is spatially uniform and
follows the analytical
constant-load solution of \citet{chengdetournay1988}.  The computed vertical
nominal-stress resultant then provides an independent check of the applied
\(100\ \mathrm{kPa}\) compression.  The right face is traction free, and the
boundaries with prescribed normal displacement are frictionless.  The
initial material state is
\(\mbf u=\mbf0\), \(p=0\), and \(\rho_s=\rho_{s0}\); the local constitutive
state then has \(\bar\rho_s=\bar\rho_{s0}\), \(\phi_s=\phi_{s0}\), and
\(\bar J=1\).  The first time step applies the analytical platen
displacement, after which its time history follows
\eqref{eq:mandel-vertical-displacement-series}.  Because the vertical
displacement is imposed and linear in \(Y\), its profile confirms the applied
boundary condition rather than providing an independent benchmark
comparison.

The solid-mass residual in \eqref{eq:solid-mass-weak-residual} evolves
\(\rho_s\) throughout the calculation.  At each integration point, the
mineral equation determines intrinsic density, the density ratio gives
\(\phi_s\), and \eqref{eq:poroplastic-biot-correction} gives \(B\) with
\(a^p=1\).  Water density follows
\eqref{eq:barotropic-fluid-density}.  The small load keeps the Biot coefficient
near its reference value while testing the nonlinear implementation.

The material parameters are listed in \cref{tab:mandel-parameters}.

\subsection{Pressure and displacement}
\label{sec:mandel-numerical-comparison}

The linearization and analytical series are given in
Appendix~\ref{sec:mandel-analytical-solution}.  The storage coefficient is
obtained from the matched logarithmic mineral law and the water law.  It
recovers the classical single-mineral storage relation, so the benchmark
tests the small-strain limit of the finite-deformation implementation.

The error and refinement study uses a \(40\times4\) mesh of nine-node
quadrilaterals with uniform backward-Euler steps
\(\Delta t=0.002\ \mathrm{s}\).  The spatial profiles use the same mesh with
a sequence with smaller time steps early in the response, beginning at \(0.001\ \mathrm{s}\) and increasing
to \(0.016\ \mathrm{s}\).
\Cref{fig:mandel-pressure-profiles} compares pressure along
\(Y=0.05\ \mathrm{m}\) at several times.  The solution reproduces the
pressure rise near the undrained center, followed by decay as fluid leaves
through the lateral boundary.

The verification evaluates pressure over the complete history at three
horizontal positions and checks the recovered nominal load.  Separate mesh

refinements reduce the pressure difference, while the mesh comparison shows
smaller spatial sensitivity on the tested grids.  These pairwise comparisons
measure sensitivity over the reported transient rather than an asymptotic
convergence order.

\subsection{Elastic compression at finite deformation}
\label{sec:mandel-finite-deformation-continuation}

A second calculation uses the same domain, phase balances, and elastic
constitutive model at a larger imposed deformation.  The platen displacement
increases linearly to \(-0.1H\) over \(0.2\ \mathrm{s}\) and is then held. The elastic calculation uses a \(40\times4\) mesh
and \(\Delta t=0.002\ \mathrm{s}\).
The pressure, lateral displacement, nominal stress, and Biot coefficient are
predictions of this displacement-controlled calculation.
The stress can be evaluated in the reduced drained form
\eqref{eq:total-stress-drained-pressure-split}, equivalent to the implemented
double-prime correction and Biot transformation.  The plotted Biot coefficient
is the reversible derivative \(B\), not the finite multiplier \(\alpha\).
This elastic calculation therefore exercises the pressure coupling before
plastic history is introduced.  The reported profiles characterize the
combined effects of constitutive pressure coupling, finite kinematics, and
fluid transport.

\begin{figure}[ht]
 \centering
 \figureinput{mandel_finite_deformation.pgf}
 \caption{Compression and drainage of the coupled elastic system at finite deformation.
 Left: imposed platen compression and predicted lateral expansion.
 Right: spatial average and range of the Biot coefficient.  The vertical
 dotted line marks the end of the displacement ramp.}
 \label{fig:mandel-finite-deformation}
\end{figure}

The Biot coefficient varies across the specimen during compression.  It briefly
exceeds the reference value in the undrained interior, while the largest
reduction occurs near the drained boundary
(\cref{fig:mandel-normalized-biot-contours}).  Drainage during the hold
reduces lateral expansion and brings the Biot coefficient toward a nearly
uniform value below its reference value.

The decrease follows directly from the relative compression of the specimen
and mineral. In the drained elastic limit, \(p=0\) and \(a^p=1\),
\eqref{eq:verification-mineral-factors} gives
\(\bar J=J^{K/(\phi_{s0}K_s)}\). Substitution into
\eqref{eq:poroplastic-biot-correction} gives
\begin{align*}
 \left.B\right|_{p=0,\,a^p=1}
 &=1-\frac{K}{K_s}J^{K/(\phi_{s0}K_s)-1},
 % equation-id: eq:drained-elastic-biot-volume
\\
 &=1-0.4J^{-5/9}
 \quad\text{for the parameters in \cref{tab:mandel-parameters}}.
 % equation-id: eq:drained-elastic-biot-parameters
\end{align*}
Since \(K/(\phi_{s0}K_s)=4/9<1\), compression increases
\(\bar J/J\): the porous specimen contracts more than the mineral, so the
solid volume fraction increases and \(B\) falls below \(B_0\).
This change is reversible in the elastic model; the Biot coefficient need not
remain constant during a finite elastic deformation.

At fixed total volume, positive pore pressure reduces the mineral volume and
increases the denominator in \eqref{eq:poroplastic-biot-correction}, raising
\(B\). This pressure contribution opposes the decrease caused by bulk
compression and accounts for the transient increase in the undrained interior.
Drainage removes this contribution while the imposed compression remains,
leaving a Biot coefficient below its initial value. The drained expression explains
the trend, while the plotted transient values use the full pressure-dependent
mineral solution.

\begin{figure}[ht]
 \centering
 \figureinput{mandel_normalized_biot_contours.pgf}
 \caption{Normalized Biot coefficient during finite compression and drainage.
 The snapshots at \(t=0.04,0.10,0.20,0.30,0.50,0.70\ \mathrm{s}\)
 share times, color limits, and panel dimensions with
 \cref{fig:poroplastic-mandel-biot-ratio}, and use solid reference coordinates.
 The largest transient reduction occurs near the drained boundary.  The vertical
 plotting scale is enlarged to show the through-height field.}
 \label{fig:mandel-normalized-biot-contours}
\end{figure}

The Biot coefficient identity and the mineral equation of state remain
satisfied during this compression and hold.  The solid-mass check measures
the error introduced when the chain-rule mass balance is discretized in
time and solved in weak form.  The resulting drift decreases as the time
step is reduced.  Mesh and time-step comparisons also check pressure, lateral
displacement, and the spatially averaged Biot coefficient.  This calculation
tests the elastic model at finite deformation.  The next calculation activates
plastic flow in the same geometry to check its contribution to fluid storage.

\subsection{Coupled poroplastic compression and fluid exchange}
\label{sec:coupled-poroplastic-mandel}

We next allow plastic pore-volume change in the same quarter-domain, using
the same elastic moduli, water properties, and boundary conditions.  The
platen again reaches ten-percent axial compression at \(0.2\ \mathrm{s}\)
and remains fixed through \(0.7\ \mathrm{s}\).  The Drucker--Prager parameters
are \(M=0.6\), \(\beta=0.4\), and \(c_0=20\ \mathrm{MPa}\), with
isotropic hardening modulus \(H=100\ \mathrm{MPa}\).
For this choice of hardening modulus, the acoustic-tensor determinant
remains positive at the sampled states and directions along the loading
path, as examined below.  The value is selected for this synthetic test
and has not been calibrated against material data.
The reference solid fraction remains \(\phi_{s0}=0.9\).
We solve the coupled poroplastic response on a \(40\times4\) mesh with
\(\Delta t=0.0025\ \mathrm{s}\).  This loading path extends the
finite-compression calculation into plastic flow.  The analytical Mandel
comparison remains a small-load elastic benchmark, while the plastic
calculation is checked through local identities, assembled derivatives,
mass balances, and refinement.

Figure~\ref{fig:poroplastic-mandel-biot-ratio} plots \(B/B_0\), where
\(B\) is the current Biot coefficient including plasticity and
\(B_0=1-K/K_s=0.6\) is its reference value.  This normalization matches
\cref{fig:mandel-normalized-biot-contours}.  The spatial distribution reflects
both the evolving mineral state and stored plastic distention.

\begin{figure}[ht]
 \centering
 \figureinput{poroplastic_mandel_biot_ratio.pgf}
 \caption{Current Biot coefficient including plasticity, normalized by its
 reference value \(B_0=1-K/K_s=0.6\), during coupled compression and fluid exchange.
 Each panel shows the element-averaged \(B/B_0\).
 The snapshot times, color scale, and panel dimensions match
 \cref{fig:mandel-normalized-biot-contours};
 a value of one denotes the reference Biot coefficient. The panels use solid
 reference coordinates. Black curves, where present, bound elements whose average plastic
 multiplier increment exceeds \(10^{-9}\).
 The vertical plotting scale is enlarged.  The element values are shown
 without smoothing.}
 \label{fig:poroplastic-mandel-biot-ratio}
\end{figure}

The pressure first rises under compression, then falls as plastic dilation
increases pore volume.  It crosses below the prescribed zero pressure at the
drained face, reversing fluid exchange from outflow to inflow
(\cref{fig:poroplastic-mandel-history}).  The pressure continues to relax
during the hold while the Biot coefficient retains the accumulated plastic
contribution.  The single-phase barotropic water law is continued into tension
in this synthetic test.  Cavitation and desaturation are not represented, so
the suction values characterize the stated constitutive model.

\begin{figure}[ht]
 \centering
 \figureinput{poroplastic_mandel_history.pgf}
 \caption{Coupled poroplastic response under a compression ramp and hold.
 Left: pressure at two positions on the symmetry edge \(Y=0\).
 Right: reference-volume averages of the current Biot coefficient and its
 virgin elastic value at the same local pressure and total volume.
 The dotted vertical line marks the end of platen motion.}
 \label{fig:poroplastic-mandel-history}
\end{figure}

Plastic dilation raises the Biot coefficient through a different mineral state.
At fixed \(J\) and \(p\), increasing \(a^p\) reduces \(J^e=J/a^p\)
and the stable mineral-volume root \(\bar J\), thereby increasing \(B\).
The resulting pore-volume evolution also changes pressure and lateral
expansion. Thus \cref{fig:mandel-normalized-biot-contours,fig:poroplastic-mandel-biot-ratio}
compare identical axial displacement histories, but different local pressure
and deformation histories. The opposite late-time changes in \(B/B_0\)
reflect both accumulated plastic distention and the accompanying changes
in the pressure and deformation fields.
They characterize the dilative flow law used here; plastic compaction would
require a different constitutive model.

To isolate the effect of plastic history, we also evaluate the virgin
elastic Biot coefficient at each plastic calculation's local pressure and total
volume.  This comparison holds the current pressure and volume fixed while
changing only the plastic history.  The difference \(B-B_{\mathrm{el}}(p,J)\) remains positive
during the hold for this dilative law. Its magnitude is a prediction of the
uncalibrated constitutive example, rather than an estimate for a particular
material.

Comparing the
\(20\times2\) and \(40\times4\) meshes at the same
\(0.0025\ \mathrm{s}\) step changes the spatial average of the difference
between the current and virgin elastic Biot coefficients by less than
\(4.0\times10^{-7}\) over the complete history.  The local field is more
sensitive: at \(0.7\ \mathrm{s}\), the root-mean-square and maximum
differences between the piecewise-constant element-average fields on the
common reference domain are \(8.3\times10^{-6}\) and
\(1.9\times10^{-5}\), respectively.  The positive plastic-history
contribution persists under these refinements.  The maximum root-mean-square
variation of this Biot coefficient difference through the specimen height is below \(10^{-14}\) over the fine-grid histories at
steps of \(0.0025\) and \(0.00125\ \mathrm{s}\).  Halving this step changes
the local Biot coefficient difference by at most \(3.9\times10^{-6}\) across the six snapshots.

A locally unique mineral solution does not by itself ensure that the
mechanical response remains stable during plastic flow.  We therefore
examine the effect of hardening on the mechanical loading tangent, which
includes the plastic deformation produced by a further load increment.
Let \(\mbf P\) denote the total first Piola stress and let \(\mbf N\) be a
unit normal in the reference plane.  We differentiate the constitutive
update during plastic loading as the load increment tends to zero, holding
pressure and the previous plastic history fixed.  This gives the loading
tangent.  Contracting it with a normal direction gives the acoustic tensor,
which we use to test for a possible loss of ellipticity.  These tensors are
\begin{align*}
 \mc A_{ijkl}
 &=\left.\frac{\mathrm dP_{ij}}{\mathrm dF_{kl}}\right|_{p,\mathrm{active}},
 % equation-id: eq:poroplastic-loading-tangent
\\
 Q_{ik}(\mbf N)&=\mc A_{ijkl}N_jN_l.
 % equation-id: eq:poroplastic-acoustic-tensor
\end{align*}
A sign change of \(\det\mbf Q\) between normal directions implies a
singular acoustic tensor at an intervening direction.  At such a direction,
the incremental equilibrium equations can admit a discontinuity in the
deformation gradient, indicating a possible onset of localized deformation.
For \(H=0\), an independent reevaluation of the constitutive loading history
gives both positive and negative determinants
at \(0.3\ \mathrm{s}\), before the pronounced late-time spatial oscillations.
At \((X,Y)=(0.5125,0.0375)\ \mathrm{m}\) and \(0.4\ \mathrm{s}\), its
minimum is \(-0.00891\ \mathrm{GPa}^2\).  The derivative of the mineral residual remains positive and far from zero.
Thus the mineral equation can still be solved locally even when the
mechanical equations lose ellipticity.  Halving the time step and, separately, setting
\(\beta=M\) while retaining \(H=0\) leave both the acoustic sign change
and the spatial oscillations.  With \(H=100\ \mathrm{MPa}\) on the
\(40\times4\) mesh at \(\Delta t=0.0025\ \mathrm{s}\), the minimum
over the sampled states undergoing plastic loading at three positions is
\(0.03797\ \mathrm{GPa}^2\), and the spatial oscillations disappear.
The diagnostic scans in-plane normal directions at \(0.25^\circ\) intervals
and checks sensitivity to the finite-difference increment.  Positive values
at these samples characterize the tested loading path.

The independent check of the water-storage derivative and both checks of
the Biot coefficient pass during plastic flow.  The comparison of the
assembled AD Jacobian with finite differences also passes through
compression and the full hold, and suppressing yielding recovers the
existing elastic solution.  On the \(20\times2\) mesh, reducing the step
from \(0.01\) to \(0.005\) and \(0.0025\ \mathrm{s}\) reduces the maximum
water-mass mismatch from
\(0.178\%\) to \(0.089\%\) and \(0.045\%\) of initial water mass.
Here the mismatch compares the change in stored water with the time
integral of the boundary flux obtained from the pressure residual.  Solid-mass
drift decreases similarly.  For the plotted \(40\times4\) calculation,
the maximum mismatch between water-mass change and integrated Darcy outflow
is \(0.291\%\) of initial water mass.  These checks verify the plastic
storage contribution while measuring the time-discretization drift and
boundary-flux spatial error separately.

\subsection{Physical interpretation, scope, and reproducibility}
\label{sec:verification-scope}

The physical question is whether the selected dilative flow law can reproduce
both fluid exchange and the evolution of the Biot coefficient in a real
material. Experiments support the pressure-reduction mechanism:
\citet{makhnenkolabuz2015} observed dilation-induced pore-pressure reduction
and increased resistance to deformation in undrained Berea sandstone.
\citet{brantut2020} measured pressure drops during shear failure of granite
and subsequent fluid influx during pressure recovery. These observations
support dilation-induced suction and fluid uptake, although their loading
and drainage conditions differ from the present calculation. The resulting
hydraulic strengthening, often termed dilatant hardening, is distinct from
the prescribed isotropic hardening of the yield surface used here.

The predicted increase in \(B\) requires a separate assessment. Measurements
on high-porosity Castlegate sandstone show that the Biot coefficient can
decrease under hydrostatic loading and stabilize after shear stress is
applied along constant-shear-stress paths \citep{ingraham2017evolution}. Those
experiments probe a different, predominantly compactive regime; they neither
validate nor rule out the increase predicted by the present dilative law.
They show that the stress path and the evolution of drained and unjacketed
stiffness matter. Observing dilation or fluid influx alone would therefore
not validate the relation between plastic distention and \(B\).

Physical assessment requires joint measurements of volume change, pore
pressure, boundary fluid exchange, and poroelastic response along matched
loading paths. The present calculations establish internal consistency and
numerical behavior for the chosen laws. They do not establish that a constant
dilation slope and the selected hardening law reproduce those observations.
The continuation of the water law into tension also needs assessment:
\citet{brantut2020} observed pressure drops reaching vapor pressure, a regime
outside the single-phase description used here.
